Part 1 · Background Reading

Debate 1

Slavery in the Cotton South: Defenders, Victims, Historians

Slavery grew rapidly after the invention of the cotton gin in 1793 and the opening of new cotton lands in the Deep South. By 1860 about four million people were enslaved in the United States. A huge domestic slave trade moved roughly a million people from the upper South to the new cotton states, and it broke up many families. At first, many white Southerners called slavery a “necessary evil” that they inherited. After the 1830s, as abolitionists gained a voice, defenders began to call it a “positive good,” a phrase John C. Calhoun used in 1837. Pro-slavery writers argued that the Bible supported slavery and that masters protected workers in a way that factory owners in the North did not.

Richard Furman, a Baptist minister in Charleston, South Carolina, wrote a defense of slavery in 1822, the year officials in his city uncovered a plot for rebellion planned by Denmark Vesey, a free Black man. George Fitzhugh, a Virginia lawyer and writer, argued in Sociology for the South (1854) that slavery was kinder than the wage labor of northern factories. Their opponents came from the people who had lived it. Harriet Jacobs, born into slavery in North Carolina in 1813, escaped in 1842 and published her story in 1861 under the name Linda Brent. In the 1930s the Federal Writers’ Project, part of the Works Progress Administration, sent interviewers to record the memories of elderly formerly enslaved people, among them Lorenzo Ivy, a Virginia man who gave his account in 1937.

Historians have fought over how to read this evidence. In 1918 Ulrich B. Phillips, a white Georgia historian, portrayed slavery as a mild and paternal system, and this picture shaped textbooks for decades. Kenneth Stampp (1956) used plantation records to show a harsh system built on violence and constant conflict, and Stanley Elkins compared slavery to a “total institution” that robbed enslaved people of their independence. Eugene Genovese and Herbert Gutman, writing in the 1970s, answered that enslaved people were not helpless: they built families, kept their religion, and resisted in ways large and small. Ask of each source which of these three views it helps most.

Debate 2

The Ideal of Separate Spheres and the Women Who Lived Beyond It

In the decades after the American Revolution, many Americans came to see the world as divided into two spheres. Men were expected to work in the public world of business and politics, while women were expected to run the home and raise moral children. Writers called this ideal the cult of domesticity, or true womanhood. Women were told that they were the guardians of religion and virtue, and that their influence would improve the nation by shaping the next generation of citizens. The ideal was most easy to follow for white, middle-class families who could afford to keep a wife at home.

It did not describe most women’s lives. Enslaved women, such as Sojourner Truth, who was born into slavery in New York and later became a traveling speaker, worked in the fields and were separated from their children. In New England textile towns, thousands of young women worked in the mills for wages. Harriet Hanson Robinson began working in the Lowell mills at age ten, and in 1836 she took part in a strike over wage cuts. At the same time, middle-class women entered reform movements as an extension of their duties as guardians of morality. They joined temperance, charitable, and antislavery groups. In 1848 the first women’s rights convention was held at Seneca Falls, New York. Lucretia Mott, a Quaker minister and abolitionist, was one of its organizers, and she argued that women’s public work followed from their moral concerns but that women also deserved equal rights.

Historians read this period in different ways. Catherine Clinton, writing in 1984, argued that women used the domestic role as a base to extend what they saw as female concerns into public life, so that the ideal strengthened women. Nancy Hewitt, in 1985, challenged the idea that all women shared one culture, because race and class divided them. She noted that a mill worker, an enslaved woman, and a middle-class reformer lived in very different worlds. Truth’s famous 1851 speech in Akron survives in more than one version, written down by different listeners, so historians treat the best-known wording with care. As you read the three sources below, ask whether each speaker shows a shared womanhood or a sharp divide.

DEBATE 1 · CASE FILES 1–4

Slavery: Paternalism, Brutality, or Resistance?

The debate:

Was slavery in the South a system of benevolent paternalism, a brutal system of control, or a world in which enslaved people made their own communities and resisted?

  • ★ PATERNALISM: Ulrich B. Phillips (1918) described slavery as a mostly gentle system in which masters cared for people they thought of as dependents. This view later became part of the “Lost Cause” story.
  • ◆ BRUTAL SYSTEM: Kenneth Stampp (1956) and Stanley Elkins argued that slavery was harsh and violent, and Elkins called it a “total institution” that damaged the people trapped in it.
  • ● COMMUNITY AND RESISTANCE: Eugene Genovese and Herbert Gutman showed that enslaved people built families, communities, and religion that let them resist and keep their dignity.
1822Case file 1 of 7
Who: Richard Furman, Baptist minister in Charleston, South Carolina
From: Published statement, Exposition of the Views of the Baptists

A pastor defends slavery on religious grounds in the year of a rebellion plot in his city.

[T]he right of holding slaves is clearly established by the Holy Scriptures, both by precept and example. . . . Had the holding of slaves been a moral evil, it cannot be supposed, that the inspired Apostles, who feared not the faces of men, and were ready to lay down their lives in the cause of their God, would have tolerated it, for a moment, in the Christian Church. . . . In proving this subject justifiable by Scriptural authority, its morality is also proved; for the Divine Law never sanctions immoral actions.
Richard Furman, Exposition of the Views of the Baptists, Relative to the Coloured Population of the United States, in a Communication to the Governor of South-Carolina (1822; 2nd ed., Charleston, 1838), excerpted at SUNY Ulster (Norton lists it under the same title, 1822).
Word help: precept = a rule of conduct · tolerated = allowed · justifiable = able to be defended · sanctions = approves · Scriptural = from the Bible

1854Case file 2 of 7
Who: George Fitzhugh, Virginia lawyer and writer
From: Book, Sociology for the South

A Virginia writer argues that slavery cares for workers better than free labor does.

We provide for each slave, in old age and in infancy, in sickness and in health, not according to his labor, but according to his wants. . . . The slaves are all well fed, well clad, have plenty of fuel, and are happy. They have no dread of the future—no fear of want. A state of dependence is the only condition in which reciprocal affection can exist among human beings . . . . Hence, though men are often found at variance with wife or children, we never saw one who did not like his slaves, and rarely a slave who was not devoted to his master.
George Fitzhugh, Sociology for the South, or the Failure of Free Society (Richmond, 1854), Appendix, pp. 245–47 (the two passages are a page or two apart; the ellipsis marks the cut), Teaching American History; checked against the 1854 edition on the Internet Archive.
Word help: clad = clothed · dread = fear · reciprocal = shared by both sides · variance = disagreement

Who: Harriet Jacobs, who escaped slavery in 1842, writing as Linda Brent
From: Autobiography, Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl, chapter 5

A woman who escaped from slavery describes her life as a teenage girl.

But I now entered on my fifteenth year—a sad epoch in the life of a slave girl. My master began to whisper foul words in my ear. Young as I was, I could not remain ignorant of their import. . . . But where could I turn for protection? No matter whether the slave girl be as black as ebony or as fair as her mistress. In either case, there is no shadow of law to protect her from insult, from violence, or even from death; all these are inflicted by fiends who bear the shape of men.

1937Case file 4 of 7
Who: Lorenzo Ivy, a formerly enslaved Virginian interviewed by a Federal Writers’ Project worker
From: Oral history interview, WPA slave narratives

An elderly man recalls a Virginia plantation seventy years after emancipation.

So old Tunstall separated families right and left. He took two of my aunts and left their husbands up here and he separated all together seven husbands and wives. One woman had twelve children. . . . Runaways! Lord, yes, they had plenty of runaways. There was two kinds of runaways: them that hid in the woods and them that run away to free land. Most slaves just runaway and hide in the woods for a week or two and then come on back. . . . They walk them there to the railroad and ship them south like cattle. Truly, son, the half has never been told.
Lorenzo Ivy, interview, 1937, Virginia Writers’ Project Life Histories, Work Projects Administration of Virginia Records, 1939–1943, Accession 30432, Federal Records Collection, Library of Virginia; re-transcribed in the Norton exercise (the original transcript used heavy dialect).
Word help: Tunstall = the man who owned Ivy’s family · runaways = people who escaped for a time or for good · the half has never been told = the full story is far worse than people know

DEBATE 2 · CASE FILES 5–7

Women in the Early Republic: Separate Spheres?

The debate:

Did the ideal of separate spheres give women of the early republic a shared identity and a source of power, or did differences of race and class divide women’s experiences?

  • ★ SHARED WOMANHOOD: Catherine Clinton (1984) argued that women took the home values of the domestic role into public life through reform.
  • ◆ DIVIDED BY RACE AND CLASS: Nancy Hewitt (1985) argued that there was no single women’s culture, because race and class divided women.
  • ● BOTH TRUE: A source that fits parts of both arguments: it uses womanhood to claim public power and also shows how experiences differed.
1849Case file 5 of 7
Who: Lucretia Mott, Quaker minister, abolitionist, and women’s rights leader
From: Speech, “Discourse on Woman”

A reformer addresses an audience in Philadelphia about women’s place in public life.

This age is notable for its works of mercy and benevolence . . . Women as well as men are interested in these works of justice and mercy. . . . We would admit all the difference, that our great and beneficent Creator has made, in the relation of man and woman, nor would we seek to disturb this relation; but we deny that the present position of woman, is her true sphere of usefulness: nor will she attain to this sphere, until the disabilities and disadvantages, religious, civil, and social, which impede her progress, are removed out of her way.
Lucretia Mott, “Discourse on Woman,” December 17, 1849, Philadelphia, Gifts of Speech, Sweet Briar College / UMass Amherst.
Word help: benevolence = kindness and charity · beneficent = doing good · disabilities = legal limits on rights · impede = slow down or block

1851Case file 6 of 7
Who: Sojourner Truth, formerly enslaved speaker and reformer
From: Speech at the Women’s Rights Convention in Akron, Ohio (Gage’s 1863 version)

A formerly enslaved woman speaks at a women’s rights convention. The words come down to us from a later account.

And ain’t I a woman? Look at me! Look at my arm! I have ploughed and planted, and gathered into barns, and no man could head me! And ain’t I a woman? I could work as much and eat as much as a man – when I could get it – and bear the lash as well! And ain’t I a woman? I have borne thirteen children, and seen most all sold off to slavery, and when I cried out with my mother’s grief, none but Jesus heard me! And ain’t I a woman?
Sojourner Truth, speech at the Women’s Rights Convention, Akron, Ohio, May 29, 1851 (the convention met May 28–29), Gifts of Speech. This is the version Frances Dana Gage published in 1863, with standard spelling in place of her dialect (“ain’t” for “a’n’t,” “the lash” for “de lash,” “children” for “chilern,” “most all” for “’em mos’ all”); the 1851 report by Marius Robinson reads very differently and does not contain “ain’t I a woman.”
Word help: ploughed = plowed, prepared fields for crops · head me = get ahead of me · the lash = a whipping

1898Case file 7 of 7
Who: Harriet Hanson Robinson, a Lowell mill worker as a girl, later an author
From: Memoir, Loom and Spindle (1898)

Decades later, a former mill worker remembers a strike from her girlhood.

One of the first strikes of cotton-factory operatives that ever took place in this country was that in Lowell, in October, 1836. When it was announced that the wages were to be cut down, great indignation was felt, and it was decided to strike, en masse. . . . One of the girls stood on a pump, and gave vent to the feelings of her companions in a neat speech, declaring that it was their duty to resist all attempts at cutting down the wages. This was the first time a woman had spoken in public in Lowell, and the event caused surprise and consternation among her audience.
Harriet Hanson Robinson, Loom and Spindle; or, Life Among the Early Mill Girls (New York: Thomas Y. Crowell & Company, 1898), 83–86, via The American Yawp.
Word help: operatives = factory workers · en masse = all together · indignation = anger at unfair treatment · consternation = shock and alarm
