Part 1 · Background Reading

The People’s President and the Limits of “the People”

When Andrew Jackson took the oath of office in March 1829, thousands of ordinary Americans crowded into Washington, and even into the White House, to celebrate. To his supporters, the moment was a victory for the “common man.” Their anger went back to 1824, when Jackson won the most popular votes and the most electoral votes but not a majority, so the House of Representatives chose John Quincy Adams instead. Jackson’s followers called it a “corrupt bargain” and spent four years building a new Democratic Party, organized by Martin Van Buren of New York, that swept Jackson into the presidency in 1828.

Jackson rode a wave of change that had started before him. In the early 1800s most states required a man to own property or pay taxes before he could vote. One state after another dropped those rules for White men. New York’s convention of 1821 shows how this worked. Delegates rejected the warnings of conservatives such as Chancellor James Kent, the state’s top judge, and opened the vote to nearly all White men. Yet the same convention raised the property requirement for Black men to $250, which shut almost all of them out. Campaigns turned into loud, popular contests with parades, barbecues, and party newspapers, and in the early 1830s parties began choosing presidential candidates at national nominating conventions instead of in closed meetings of congressmen.

Once in office, Jackson claimed to speak for “the people” against privileged insiders. He practiced “rotation in office,” replacing about one-fifth of federal officeholders with his own supporters, a practice his critics called the spoils system. He also went to war against the Second Bank of the United States, the nation’s largest corporation, which held the government’s money and issued much of the country’s paper currency. In 1832 Jackson vetoed the bill to renew the Bank’s charter, and he won reelection easily that fall. He then moved federal deposits into favored state banks. Those banks lent freely and land speculation boomed. When British lenders tightened credit and cotton prices fell, the Panic of 1837 began a depression that lasted for years. Jackson’s opponents, who mocked him as “King Andrew the First,” had already formed the Whig Party in 1834, and the contest between Democrats and Whigs created a two-party system that lasted about twenty years.

Jackson also defended the Union when a state challenged federal power. South Carolina, angry over the high Tariff of 1828 and afraid that a strong national government might someday threaten slavery, declared the tariffs “null, void, and no law” in 1832. Jackson called nullification treason and asked Congress for the Force Bill, which allowed him to use the army to enforce federal law. Congress passed it in 1833 along with Henry Clay’s compromise tariff, so both sides could claim victory.

The limits of Jacksonian democracy were sharpest for people outside the circle of White men. Women could not vote. In 1830 Jackson signed the Indian Removal Act, which let him trade western land for the eastern homelands of Indigenous nations, home to nearly 100,000 people. The Cherokee, who had a written constitution, a newspaper, and farms, fought removal in court. In Worcester v. Georgia (1832) the Supreme Court ruled that Georgia’s laws had no force inside the Cherokee Nation, but Jackson did nothing to enforce the ruling. In 1838 and 1839 the army forced about 17,000 Cherokee west on the Trail of Tears, and thousands died. That same year, Pennsylvania adopted a new constitution that widened voting for White men but added the word “white” to its voting clause, taking the ballot from Black men who had voted there for decades. Black Pennsylvanians did not win it back until the Fifteenth Amendment in 1870.

The Age of Jackson leaves a puzzle. More men took part in politics than ever before, yet in other ways the meaning of “the people” grew narrower. As you open each case file, ask who the author counts as “the people” and who is left out.

1821Case file 1 of 5
Who: Chancellor James Kent, New York’s highest-ranking judge
From: Speech at the New York Constitutional Convention

At New York’s 1821 convention, delegates debated whether a man had to own land to vote for the state senate.

The notion that every man that works a day on the road, or serves an idle hour in the militia, is entitled as of right to an equal participation in the whole power of the government, is most unreasonable, and has no foundation in justice. . . . Society is an association for the protection of property as well as of life, and the individual who contributes only one cent to the common stock, ought not to have the same power and influence in directing the property concerns of the partnership, as he who contributes his thousands.
James Kent, speech in the New York Constitutional Convention, September 1821, in Reports of the Proceedings and Debates of the Convention of 1821, reported by Nathaniel H. Carter, William L. Stone, and Marcus T. C. Gould (Albany: E. and E. Hosford, 1821). Garza Archive P10.1.
Word help: militia = citizen soldiers · participation = a share · common stock = the shared fund (here, society’s wealth) · partnership = a business owned by several people

1829Case file 2 of 5
Who: President Andrew Jackson
From: First Annual Message to Congress

In his first yearly message to Congress, the new president asked for changes in how presidents are chosen and how government jobs are held.

To the people belongs the right of electing their Chief Magistrate; it was never designed that their choice should in any case be defeated, either by the intervention of electoral colleges or by the agency confided, under certain contingencies, to the House of Representatives. . . . So far, therefore, as the people can with convenience speak, it is safer for them to express their own will. . . . In a country where offices are created solely for the benefit of the people no one man has any more intrinsic right to official station than another.
Andrew Jackson, “First Annual Message,” December 8, 1829, American Presidency Project, ed. Gerhard Peters and John T. Woolley. Garza Archive P10.9.
Word help: Chief Magistrate = the president · electoral colleges = electors who formally pick the president · contingencies = special situations · intrinsic = built-in · official station = a government job

1830Case file 3 of 5
Who: President Andrew Jackson
From: Second Annual Message to Congress (removal section)

In his second yearly message, Jackson told Congress what the removal of Indigenous nations would bring.

It will place a dense and civilized population in large tracts of country now occupied by a few savage hunters. . . . It will relieve the whole State of Mississippi and the western part of Alabama of Indian occupancy, and enable those States to advance rapidly in population, wealth, and power. It will separate the Indians from immediate contact with settlements of whites; free them from the power of the States; enable them to pursue happiness in their own way and under their own rude institutions . . .
Andrew Jackson, “Second Annual Message,” December 6, 1830, in A Compilation of the Messages and Papers of the Presidents, ed. James D. Richardson, vol. 2 (Washington, DC: Government Printing Office, 1896). Garza Archive P10.19.
Word help: dense = crowded · savage = Jackson’s insulting word for Indigenous people · occupancy = living on and holding land · rude = simple, rough (another insult)

1832Case file 4 of 5
Who: President Andrew Jackson
From: Veto message to the Senate

Jackson explains to the Senate why he will not sign the bill renewing the charter of the Second Bank of the United States.

It is to be regretted that the rich and powerful too often bend the acts of government to their selfish purposes. . . . [W]hen the laws undertake to add to these natural and just advantages artificial distinctions, to grant titles, gratuities, and exclusive privileges, to make the rich richer and the potent more powerful, the humble members of society—the farmers, mechanics, and laborers—who have neither the time nor the means of securing like favors to themselves, have a right to complain of the injustice of their Government.
Andrew Jackson, “Veto Message Regarding the Bank of the United States,” July 10, 1832, Avalon Project, Yale Law School. Garza Archive P10.27.
Word help: artificial distinctions = special ranks the law creates · gratuities = gifts · exclusive privileges = special rights only a few get · potent = powerful · mechanics = skilled workers such as carpenters

1838Case file 5 of 5
Who: Robert Purvis, chairman, for a committee of Black Philadelphians
From: Public appeal to the voters of Pennsylvania

A committee of Black Philadelphians writes to the voters who will decide whether to approve Pennsylvania’s new constitution.

We appeal to you from the decision of the “Reform Convention,” which has stripped us of a right peaceably enjoyed during forty-seven years under the Constitution of this commonwealth. . . . Was it made the business of the Convention to deny “that all men are born equally free,” by making political rights depend upon the skin in which a man is born? or to divide what our fathers bled to unite, to wit, TAXATION and REPRESENTATION?
Robert Purvis et al., Appeal of Forty Thousand Citizens, Threatened with Disfranchisement, to the People of Pennsylvania (Philadelphia: Merrihew and Gunn, 1838), 3. Garza Archive P10.64.
Word help: commonwealth = the state (Pennsylvania) · Reform Convention = the meeting that rewrote Pennsylvania’s constitution · to wit = namely · disfranchisement = losing the right to vote
