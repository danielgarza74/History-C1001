Part 1 · Background Reading

A Law, a Policy, and a Trail of Tears

In the early 1800s the Cherokee, Creek, Choctaw, Chickasaw, and Seminole nations still held large lands in the Southeast, inside the borders of states such as Georgia, Alabama, and Mississippi. Many of these peoples had signed treaties with the United States that promised their borders. The Cherokee went further. They wrote a constitution in 1827, published a newspaper, the Cherokee Phoenix, in English and in their own alphabet, and called themselves a nation with a government of its own. Georgia claimed all of that land for itself, and in 1830 its laws were extended over Cherokee territory, which meant Cherokees lost many legal rights. White settlers wanted the farmland, and the discovery of gold on Cherokee land in Georgia made the pressure worse.

Andrew Jackson, who had fought Native nations in the Southeast as a general, became president in 1829 and made removal a priority. Congress passed the Indian Removal Act on May 28, 1830, which offered Native nations land west of the Mississippi in exchange for their eastern homes, and on December 6, 1830, in his second annual message to Congress, Jackson defended the policy. The vote in the House was close, 102 to 97, and the law did not say that anyone could be forced to go; it authorized the president to negotiate exchanges. The Choctaw signed the first removal treaty in 1830. In practice, though, the pressure from states, settlers, and federal agents was heavy, and most nations in the Southeast were moved west during the 1830s. Cherokee leaders fought back with petitions, a Supreme Court case, and public appeals. In Worcester v. Georgia (1832) the Court held that Georgia’s laws could not be enforced on Cherokee land, yet Georgia ignored the decision and the federal government did not enforce it. John Ross, the Cherokee principal chief, argued that treaties guaranteed their land. A small group of Cherokees signed the Treaty of New Echota in 1835 giving up the land, although most of the nation rejected it. In 1838 and 1839 federal troops rounded up some 16,000 Cherokees and marched them west on the Trail of Tears, and about 4,000 of them died.

Supporters and critics argued about the policy from the start. Lewis Cass, then governor of Michigan Territory and later Jackson’s Secretary of War, wrote in 1830 that removal would be humane, while Jeremiah Evarts, a missionary leader in Boston, argued in 1829 under the name “William Penn” that it was unjust. Historians still disagree. Oscar Handlin, Theda Perdue, and Anthony Wallace have argued that Jackson’s own racism and that of his supporters drove the policy. Robert Remini and Francis Paul Prucha have defended Jackson, saying he believed removal would protect Native people from state governments and from destruction. Alfred A. Cave takes a third position: the law never allowed force, so the use of it, and the broken treaties, were an abuse of power by the government. As you read each source, ask whose argument it helps the most.

DEBATE 1 · CASE FILES 1–4

Indian Removal: Hostility, Humanity, or Abuse of Power?

The debate:

Why did the United States force Native nations west of the Mississippi in the 1830s: because Andrew Jackson and white Americans were hostile to Native people, because Jackson sincerely tried to protect them, or because the government abused its power and broke its own treaties?

  • ★ HOSTILE POLICY: Oscar Handlin, Theda Perdue, and Anthony Wallace argue that Jackson’s hostility and the racism of his white supporters drove removal.
  • ◆ PROTECTIVE POLICY: Robert Remini and Francis Paul Prucha argue that Jackson believed removal would save Native peoples from the states and from destruction.
  • ● ABUSE OF POWER: Alfred A. Cave argues that the Removal Act did not authorize force and that treaties were broken, so officials abused their power.
1829Case file 1 of 4
Who: Jeremiah Evarts, a missionary leader who wrote as “William Penn”
From: Newspaper essay opposing removal

A Boston missionary leader defends Native land rights in a series of essays.

The American Indians, now living upon lands derived from their ancestors, and never alienated nor surrendered, have a perfect right to the continued and undisturbed possession of these lands. . . . These rights of soil and of sovereignty are inherent in the Indians, till voluntarily surrendered by them; and cannot be taken away by compacts between communities of whites, to which compacts the Indians were not a party. . . .
William Penn [Jeremiah Evarts], “A Brief View of the Present Relations between the Government and People of the United States and the Indians within Our National Limits” (1829), as printed in the Southern Galaxy (Natchez, Miss.), January 7, 1830, p. 1; also in In Time and Place, from Theda Perdue and Michael D. Green, eds., The Cherokee Removal: A Brief History with Documents (1995). Ellipses mark Norton’s omissions; Perdue and Green (pp. 96–98) date the essay November 1829.
Word help: alienated = sold or given up · sovereignty = the right of a nation to govern itself · inherent = belonging by nature · compacts = agreements

1830Case file 2 of 4
Who: Lewis Cass, governor of Michigan Territory (Jackson’s Secretary of War from 1831)
From: Article, “Removal of the Indians,” in a Boston review

A governor who later joined Jackson’s cabinet explains in print why he thinks removal can be carried out kindly.

. . . in this transplantation from the soil of their ancestors to the plains of the Mississippi, some mental and corporeal sufferings await the emigrants. These are inseparable from the measure itself. But by an appropriation liberally made, and prudently applied, the journey may be rendered as easy for them, as for an equal number of our own people. . . . Let the whole subject be fully explained to the Indians. Let them know that the establishment of an independent government is a hopeless project . . . .
Lewis Cass, “Removal of the Indians,” North American Review, January 1830, National Humanities Center.
Word help: transplantation = moving to a new place · corporeal = of the body · emigrants = people who leave a country · appropriation = money set aside by law · inseparable = cannot be separated

1830Case file 3 of 4
Who: The General Council of the Cherokee Nation (its Principal Chief, John Ross, is credited as the main author)
From: Public address to the people of the United States

In the summer after the law passes, the Cherokee government speaks to the American public.

We are not willing to remove; and if we could be brought to this extremity, it would be not by argument, not because our judgment was satisfied, not because our condition will be improved; but only because we cannot endure to be deprived of our national and individual rights and subjected to a process of intolerable oppression. . . . The treaties with us, and laws of the United States made in pursuance of treaties, guaranty our residence, and our privileges and secure us against intruders. Our only request is, that these treaties may be fulfilled, and these laws executed.
“Address of the Committee and Council of the Cherokee Nation in General Council Convened, to the People of the United States,” signed by Lewis Ross (president of the committee), Going Snake (speaker of the council), and other members; Cherokee Phoenix, July 24, 1830, p. 1 (transcription, Hunter Library, Western Carolina University); also Teaching American History, Ashbrook Center.
Word help: extremity = a desperate point · guaranty = guarantee · intruders = people who enter land without permission

1830Case file 4 of 4
Who: President Andrew Jackson
From: Second annual message to Congress, December 6, 1830

The president defends the Indian Removal Act to Congress.

It will place a dense and civilized population in large tracts of country now occupied by a few savage hunters. . . . It will separate the Indians from immediate contact with settlements of whites; free them from the power of the States; enable them to pursue happiness in their own way and under their own rude institutions . . . . Rightly considered, the policy of the General Government toward the red man is not only liberal, but generous. He is unwilling to submit to the laws of the States and mingle with their population. To save him from this alternative, or perhaps utter annihilation, the General Government kindly offers him a new home . . . .
Andrew Jackson, Second Annual Message to Congress, December 6, 1830, National Archives, Milestone Documents.
Word help: dense = crowded together · alternative = the other choice · annihilation = complete destruction · rude = simple, undeveloped (Jackson’s word)
