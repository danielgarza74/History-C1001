Part 1 · Background Reading

Why Did Reconstruction End?

When the Civil War ended in 1865, about four million formerly enslaved people were free, and the nation faced hard questions: What rights would freed people have? Who would own the land? How would the Southern states return to the Union? The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery in December 1865. Congress had created the Freedmen’s Bureau that March to help freed people with food, schools, and labor contracts. It was led by General Oliver Otis Howard. Early in 1865 General William T. Sherman had set aside coastal land in South Carolina, Georgia, and Florida for freed families, and thousands settled there on plots of up to forty acres.

After Abraham Lincoln’s assassination, President Andrew Johnson pardoned most former Confederates and ordered most of that land returned to its former owners. In October 1865, freedmen on Edisto Island, South Carolina, heard this news from General Howard and sent a petition to the president asking to keep or buy their land. They did not get it. Southern legislatures also passed “Black codes” that restricted where freed people could work and live. Congress answered with the Fourteenth Amendment, which defined citizenship and promised equal protection, and with the Reconstruction Acts of 1867, which divided the South into military districts. The Fifteenth Amendment (1870) said the vote could not be denied because of race. For a time, Black men voted in large numbers and held offices from town councils to Congress.

White resistance began early. The Ku Klux Klan formed in Tennessee in 1866 and spread across the South, attacking Black and white Republicans to keep them from voting and organizing. Congress passed Enforcement Acts in 1870 and 1871, and in 1871 a joint committee held hearings in South Carolina, where victims and their families, including Charlotte Fowler, testified about night-time attacks.

Northern support weakened. The Panic of 1873 began a long depression, scandals hurt the Republican Party, and many Northerners grew tired of Reconstruction. Southern Democrats, who called themselves Redeemers, won back state after state. In Mississippi in 1875, armed white men known as “white liners” attacked Republican gatherings, including a barbecue in Clinton on September 4. Governor Adelbert Ames, a Republican from Maine and a former Union general, asked President Ulysses S. Grant for troops. Grant refused. After the disputed election of 1876, the Compromise of 1877 gave Rutherford B. Hayes the presidency and the last federal troops left the South. Most freedpeople, without land of their own, became sharecroppers who farmed another person’s land for a share of the crop. In 1879 and 1880 thousands of Black southerners left for Kansas, and a United States Senate committee in 1880 investigated why. R. B. Avery, a white man from the North who had settled in Georgia, testified.

Historians still argue about what ended Reconstruction. In 1988 Eric Foner concluded that Reconstruction “failed,” but for many reasons: the depression and the banking system limited economic change, the refusal to give freed people land left a powerful planter class in place, Republicans suffered from factionalism and corruption and could not win over white voters, violence turned elections, and Northern resolve faded. In 2023 Kidada E. Williams argued that the word “failure” hides what happened. Using the stories of Black survivors, she showed that white conservatives waged a paramilitary war against Reconstruction and overthrew it. As you read, ask which argument each source helps, and remember that some sources help both.

1865Case file 1 of 4
Who: Henry Bram, Ishmael Moultrie, and Yates Sampson, a committee of freedmen on Edisto Island, South Carolina
From: Petition to President Andrew Johnson

Formerly enslaved people react to news that their land may be given back to its former owners.

This is our home, we have made These lands what they are. we were the only true and Loyal people that were found in posession of these Lands. . . . Shall not we who Are freedman and have been always true to this Union have the same rights as are enjoyed by Others?  . . . Have we forfieted our rights of property In Land?– If not then! are not our rights as A free people and good citizens of these United States To be considered before the rights of those who were Found in rebellion against this good and just Goverment . . . We are ready to pay for this land When Government calls for it.
Henry Bram et al. to the President of these United States, Edisto Island, S.C., October 28, 1865, in Freedom: A Documentary History of Emancipation, Series 3, Vol. 1: Land and Labor, 1865, Freedmen and Southern Society Project, University of Maryland. Original spelling kept.
Word help: posession = possession · forfieted = forfeited, given up · rebellion = the Confederate rebellion · Goverment = government

1871Case file 2 of 4
Who: Charlotte Fowler, a Black woman in Spartanburg County, South Carolina
From: Testimony before the Joint Select Committee of Congress on Ku Klux outrages

A widow answers questions from members of Congress about the death of her husband.

Question. How long ago is it since your husband was killed? Answer. It was the 1st of May. . . . Question. What are these men called that go about masked in that way? Answer. I don’t know; they call them Ku-Klux. . . . Question. Did your old man belong to any party? Answer. Yes, sir. Question. What party? Answer. The radicals. . . . Question. Are the colored people afraid of these people that go masked? Answer. Yes, sir; they are as afraid as death of them. There is now a whole procession of people that have left their houses and are lying out.
Testimony of Charlotte Fowler, Spartanburgh, S.C., July 6, 1871, in Testimony Taken by the Joint Select Committee to Enquire into the Condition of Affairs in the Late Insurrectionary States, South Carolina, vol. 3 (Washington, DC: Government Printing Office, 1872), 386–392, via Teaching American History.
Word help: masked = wearing a mask to hide a face · the radicals = Radical Republicans, the party of Black voters · lying out = sleeping outdoors in hiding

1875Case file 3 of 4
Who: Adelbert Ames, Republican governor of Mississippi
From: Letter to his wife, Blanche Butler Ames

The governor writes the day after a deadly attack on a Republican gathering.

There were present at a Republican barbecue about fifteen hundred colored people, men, women and children. Seeking the opportunity white men, fully prepared, fired into this crowd. . . . Last night, this morning and today squads of white men are scouring the county killing Negroes. . . . This is but in keeping with the programme of the Democracy at this time. They know we have a majority of some thirty thousand and to overcome it they are resorting to intimidation and murder. . . . I shall at once try to get troops from the general government. Of course it will be a difficult thing to do.
Adelbert Ames to Blanche Butler Ames, Jackson, Miss., September 5, 1875, in Chronicles from the Nineteenth Century: Family Letters of Blanche Butler and Adelbert Ames, vol. 2 (privately printed, 1957), via the Internet Archive.
Word help: programme = plan · the Democracy = the Democratic Party · intimidation = scaring people into obeying · squads = small armed groups

1880Case file 4 of 4
Who: R. B. Avery, a white man from the North who settled in Georgia
From: Testimony before a United States Senate committee

A witness describes the homes of Black farmers he visited in Georgia.

I do not say that what I saw was a fair average, but I went to the homes of perhaps one hundred and fifty different men. In all of these houses I found but two chairs—just two chairs in all of them. . . . I thought I had seen poverty in the great cities of the country and elsewhere, but I never saw anything to compare with the poverty of those negroes there. . . . The claims of the colored people were for property taken in 1865. They had more in 1865 than they have had since, and I think their claims amounted to more than the entire value of the whole county now.
Testimony of R. B. Avery, Washington, D.C., March 17, 1880, Report and Testimony of the Select Committee of the U.S. Senate to Investigate the Causes of the Removal of the Negroes from the Southern States to the Northern States, Part II, 46th Congress, 2nd Session, Report 693 (Washington: Government Printing Office, 1880), 260–261.
Word help: claims = requests for payment or return of property · property taken = goods seized from freedpeople in 1865 · negroes = the period word for Black people
