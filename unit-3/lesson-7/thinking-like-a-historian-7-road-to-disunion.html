Part 1 · Background Reading

Liberty, Land, and Slavery in Texas

In 1821 Mexico won its independence from Spain and inherited Texas, a huge, thinly settled northern province. Mexican leaders wanted more settlers there, partly to hold off Comanche raids and partly to build up the economy, so they offered cheap land to newcomers who promised to obey Mexican law. Thousands of Americans accepted. Stephen F. Austin, who led the first colony in the early 1820s, became the most famous of the contractors, called empresarios, who recruited families. Most settlers came from the southern states, and many brought enslaved people with them. Texas was also joined to Coahuila as a single Mexican state, with a capital hundreds of miles away, and settlers often felt outvoted and badly served by distant courts.

Mexican officials began to worry. In 1828 General Manuel de Mier y Terán, a Mexican army officer, toured East Texas and reported on what he saw. He warned that Mexican influence faded the farther one traveled toward Nacogdoches, and that Americans were pressing to keep slavery in a country that was moving against it. Mexico moved to end slavery in 1829, and in 1830 it tried to stop new immigration from the United States. Neither effort worked well, and Anglo-American settlers kept coming.

The crisis came in the mid-1830s, when President Antonio López de Santa Anna threw out Mexico’s federal Constitution of 1824 and built a much stronger central government. Austin, who had spent months in a Mexican jail after pressing for a separate Texas state, came home in the fall of 1835 and war began that October. On March 2, 1836, delegates meeting at Washington, Texas, declared independence. Four days later the Mexican army stormed the Alamo mission in San Antonio and killed nearly all of its defenders. On April 21 Sam Houston’s army defeated Santa Anna at San Jacinto, and Texas became an independent republic. Its new constitution protected slavery, and its leaders hoped the United States would annex it. Annexation finally came in 1845 and helped lead to the Mexican-American War.

Historians disagree about why Texans rebelled. In the early twentieth century, scholars such as Eugene Barker told the story as a heroic defense of liberty: freedom-loving settlers resisting a tyrant. T. R. Fehrenbach’s 1968 book Lone Star kept that heroic story popular. More recent historians, among them Gregg Cantrell, Randolph Campbell, and Sam Haynes, point instead to several causes working together: Anglo-American immigration, economic and land interests, and the role of slavery. They also remind readers that Tejanos, the Mexican-born residents of Texas, had a stake in the fight and were not all on one side.

Even at the time, people disagreed. Benjamin Lundy, an antislavery newspaper editor who had traveled in Texas, argued in 1836 that the revolt was a scheme by slaveholders to take Texas from Mexico. He was an eyewitness, but he was also a committed opponent of slavery, and a good historian reads every source with that in mind. As you work, ask which interpretation each document helps most, and remember that some help both.

1828Case file 1 of 4
Who: General Manuel de Mier y Terán, Mexican army officer
From: Letter to President Guadalupe Victoria of Mexico

A Mexican general reports to the president after traveling through the Texas settlements.

As one covers the distance from Béjar to this town, he will note that Mexican influence is proportionately diminished until on arriving in [Nacogdoches] he will see that it is almost nothing. . . . I am warning you to take timely measures. Texas could throw the whole nation into revolution. . . . The wealthy Americans of Louisiana and other western states are anxious to secure land in Texas for speculation, but they are restrained by the laws prohibiting slavery. . . . The repeal of these laws is a point toward which the colonists are directing their efforts.
Manuel de Mier y Terán to President Guadalupe Victoria, Nacogdoches, June 30, 1828, English translation as printed in Alleine Howren, “Causes and Origin of the Decree of April 6, 1830,” Southwestern Historical Quarterly 16, no. 4 (April 1913): 378–422, at 395–97 (translator not named; Howren says the surviving transcript is unsigned but attributes it to Terán).
Word help: Béjar = San Antonio · proportionately diminished = shrinks step by step · speculation = buying land hoping to sell it for profit · repeal = cancel a law

1835Case file 2 of 4
Who: Stephen F. Austin, leader of the first American colony in Texas
From: Letter to his cousin Mary Austin Holley

Austin writes from New Orleans a few weeks before the fighting begins.

It is very evident that the best interests of the United States require that Texas should be effectually, and fully, Americanized—that is—settled by a population that will harmonize with their neighbors on the East, in language, political principles, common origin, sympathy, and even interest. Texas must be a slave country. It is no longer a matter of doubt. The interest of Louisiana requires that it should be, a population of fanatical abolitionists in Texas would have a very pernicious and dangerous influence on the overgrown slave population of that state. . . . A gentle breeze shakes off a ripe peach.
Stephen F. Austin to Mary Austin Holley, New Orleans, August 21, 1835, in The Austin Papers, ed. Eugene C. Barker, vol. 3 (Austin: University of Texas Press, 1927), 101–103; also in Papers of the Texas Revolution, vol. 1.
Word help: effectually = in effect · harmonize = fit in, get along · pernicious = harmful · abolitionists = people who wanted to end slavery

1836Case file 3 of 4
Who: Delegates of the Convention of Texas
From: Texas Declaration of Independence

Delegates explain to “an impartial world” why they are leaving Mexico.

The Mexican government, by its colonization laws, invited and induced the Anglo-American population of Texas to colonize its wilderness under the pledged faith of a written constitution, that they should continue to enjoy that constitutional liberty and republican government to which they had been habituated in the land of their birth, the United States of America. In this expectation they have been cruelly disappointed . . . General Antonio Lopez de Santa Anna, who having overturned the constitution of his country, now offers us the cruel alternative, either to abandon our homes . . . or submit to the most intolerable of all tyranny, the combined despotism of the sword and the priesthood.
“The Unanimous Declaration of Independence made by the Delegates of the People of Texas in General Convention at the town of Washington on the 2nd day of March 1836,” Avalon Project, Yale Law School.
Word help: colonization laws = laws that invited settlers · habituated = used to · acquiesced = went along with · despotism = harsh one-man rule · priesthood = the Catholic Church

1836Case file 4 of 4
Who: Benjamin Lundy, antislavery newspaper editor
From: “The War in Texas,” antislavery pamphlet

An editor who had traveled in Texas tells Northern readers what he thinks the war is really about.

They have been induced to believe that the inhabitants of Texas were engaged in a legitimate contest for the maintenance of the sacred principles of Liberty . . . whereas, the motives of its instigators . . . have been from the commencement, of a directly opposite character and tendency. It is susceptible of the clearest demonstration that the immediate cause and the leading object of this contest originated in a settled design, among the slaveholders of this country . . . to wrest the large and valuable territory of Texas from the Mexican Republic, in order to re-establish the SYSTEM OF SLAVERY; to open a vast and profitable SLAVE-MARKET therein; and, ultimately, to annex it to the United States.
Benjamin Lundy, The War in Texas (Philadelphia: Merrihew and Gunn, 1836), as reprinted in Thomas Earle, ed., The Life, Travels and Opinions of Benjamin Lundy (1847); text from the Sons of DeWitt Colony Texas website.
Word help: induced = led · legitimate = lawful, fair · instigators = people who stir up a conflict · wrest = pull away by force · annex = add to a country
