Part 1 · Background Reading

A War Over What?

Abraham Lincoln won the presidency in November 1860 without carrying a single Southern state. He belonged to the new Republican Party, which opposed spreading slavery into the western territories. Lincoln promised not to interfere with slavery where it already existed, but many Southern leaders did not believe him. They feared that a Republican president would keep slavery out of new territories, and that a slavery confined to the South would slowly lose political power and eventually be put on a path to ending. On December 20, 1860, South Carolina voted to leave the Union, and four days later its convention published a Declaration of the Immediate Causes explaining why. By the first days of February 1861, six more states of the Deep South had followed: Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas. Texas held a convention that approved secession on February 1 and adopted its own declaration of causes the next day.

In February 1861 the seven states formed the Confederate States of America. Jefferson Davis, a wealthy Mississippi planter and former United States senator, became its president, and Alexander Stephens of Georgia became vice president. On March 21, 1861, Stephens spoke in Savannah, Georgia, to explain the new Confederate Constitution to a crowd of supporters. Fighting began on April 12, when Confederate guns fired on Fort Sumter in Charleston harbor, and four more states then joined the Confederacy.

The war did not stay a simple fight over secession. Lincoln began by saying that he was fighting to save the Union. But enslaved people ran to Union lines by the thousands, the army needed more soldiers, and the Confederacy depended on enslaved labor. On January 1, 1863, Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation, which declared the enslaved people in Confederate-held territory free and opened the Union army to Black soldiers. About 180,000 Black men eventually served in it, and the Union’s purpose grew from restoring the old nation to building a new one without slavery. After the Confederate surrender at Appomattox in April 1865, the Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery everywhere in the country. At least 620,000 soldiers died.

Almost as soon as the guns stopped, people began arguing about what the war had been about. Jefferson Davis, captured in 1865 and held for two years, later wrote a long defense of the Confederate cause, The Rise and Fall of the Confederate Government, published in 1881. Writers like him helped build the “Lost Cause” story, which presented the South as a defender of constitutional liberty. Historians in the early twentieth century, including Charles and Mary Beard and Frank Owsley, emphasized an economic clash between Northern industry and Southern plantation agriculture, and they accepted that states’ rights mattered to the South. Later historians, including David Potter, Eric Foner, and James McPherson, put slavery at the center. They point to the quarrels over slavery in new territories that followed the Mexican War, the failed compromises of the 1850s, and the election of 1860, and they argue that the economic and cultural differences between the regions were themselves rooted in slavery.

When you read the four case files, notice who is speaking, when, and to whom. A speech to supporters in 1861 and a memoir written sixteen years after the war may tell very different stories, and a good historian asks why.

1860Case file 1 of 4
Who: The South Carolina secession convention
From: Declaration of the Immediate Causes Which Induce and Justify the Secession of South Carolina

South Carolina explains its vote to leave the Union, pointing to what the Constitution requires of the states.

We maintain that in every compact between two or more parties, the obligation is mutual; that the failure of one of the contracting parties to perform a material part of the agreement, entirely releases the obligation of the other . . . For many years these laws were executed. But an increasing hostility on the part of the non-slaveholding States to the institution of slavery, has led to a disregard of their obligations . . . Thus the constituted compact has been deliberately broken and disregarded by the non-slaveholding States, and the consequence follows that South Carolina is released from her obligation.
“Declaration of the Immediate Causes Which Induce and Justify the Secession of South Carolina from the Federal Union,” December 24, 1860, Avalon Project, Yale Law School.
Word help: compact = agreement between equals · material = important · these laws = laws that required states to return people who escaped slavery · non-slaveholding States = the free states · released from her obligation = free to leave

1861Case file 2 of 4
Who: The Texas secession convention
From: A Declaration of the Causes which Impel the State of Texas to Secede from the Federal Union

Texas lists what it blames on the free states and states its own beliefs.

They demand the abolition of negro slavery throughout the confederacy, the recognition of political equality between the white and negro races, and avow their determination to press on their crusade against us, so long as a negro slave remains in these States. . . . We hold as undeniable truths that the governments of the various States, and of the confederacy itself, were established exclusively by the white race, for themselves and their posterity; that the African race had no agency in their establishment; that they were rightfully held and regarded as an inferior and dependent race . . .
“A Declaration of the Causes which Impel the State of Texas to Secede from the Federal Union,” adopted February 2, 1861, Avalon Project, Yale Law School. The document’s racist language reflects its authors’ white supremacist views.
Word help: abolition = ending slavery · avow = openly declare · crusade = a hard campaign for a cause · posterity = descendants · agency = a part in making something

1861Case file 3 of 4
Who: Alexander H. Stephens, vice president of the Confederacy
From: Speech in Savannah, Georgia (the “Corner Stone” speech)

Stephens explains to a Georgia crowd what is new about the Confederate Constitution.

The new constitution has put at rest, forever, all the agitating questions relating to our peculiar institution—African slavery as it exists amongst us—the proper status of the negro in our form of civilization. This was the immediate cause of the late rupture and present revolution. . . . Our new government is founded upon exactly the opposite idea; its foundations are laid, its corner-stone rests, upon the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery, subordination to the superior race, is his natural and normal condition.
Alexander H. Stephens, speech at the Athenaeum, Savannah, Georgia, March 21, 1861, in Henry Cleveland, ed., Alexander H. Stephens, in Public and Private (Philadelphia: National Publishing Company, 1866), 717–729, via Teaching American History. Some reprints read “moral” in place of “normal.”
Word help: peculiar institution = a polite name for slavery · rupture = break · agitating = stirring up trouble · subordination = being ranked beneath others

1881Case file 4 of 4
Who: Jefferson Davis, former president of the Confederacy
From: The Rise and Fall of the Confederate Government, a memoir

Sixteen years after the war, Davis looks back at its causes.

[T]o whatever extent the question of slavery may have served as an occasion, it was far from being the cause of the conflict. . . . [T]he sectional hostility . . . was not the consequence of any difference on the abstract question of slavery. It was the offspring of sectional rivalry and political ambition. It would have manifested itself just as certainly if slavery had existed in all the States, or if there had not been a negro in America. . . . The truth remains intact and incontrovertible, that the existence of African servitude was in no wise the cause of the conflict, but only an incident.
Jefferson Davis, The Rise and Fall of the Confederate Government, vol. 1 (New York: D. Appleton, 1881), ch. 10; text via civilwar.com’s transcription of the 1881 edition.
Word help: occasion = a moment that sets something off · sectional = about regions · abstract = a matter of ideas · incontrovertible = cannot be argued against · incident = a minor event
