Part 1 · Background Reading

A Revolution for Whom?

The Declaration of Independence, adopted in 1776, announced that “all men are created equal” and that governments get their power from the consent of the governed. Historians have argued ever since about how far those words changed American life. Gordon Wood is the best known voice for the “radical” view. In his account the war overturned monarchy and a society built on inherited rank, and it created a republic in which the people were the source of authority. Wood sums up his view by calling the Revolution the most radical and far-reaching event in American history.

Other historians see far less change. The men who called for independence were mostly the same elites who led the new states and, later, the nation. Slavery survived and grew in the South, women remained legally subordinate, and the peace treaty that ended the war in 1783 opened western lands to white settlement without regard for Native nations that had fought on both sides. A third group of historians shifts the focus to people usually left out of the story. Enslaved and free Black Americans, women, and Native Americans took part in the war, often on both sides, and many of them borrowed the language of natural rights to demand freedom, equality, or sovereignty for themselves.

Three kinds of evidence help sort this out. First, the new state governments. Pennsylvania adopted a constitution in September 1776 that gave the vote to nearly every free man who paid taxes. South Carolina’s 1778 constitution, written by a planter elite, limited voting to free white men who owned land or paid a substantial tax and required officeholders to own large amounts of property. Second, petitions and letters from people who were shut out. In January 1777 Prince Hall, a free Black man in Boston, joined with other enslaved men to ask the Massachusetts legislature to abolish slavery, arguing that the principles Americans used against Britain applied to them as well. In the spring of 1776 Abigail Adams, writing from her farm in Braintree, Massachusetts, urged her husband John, a delegate to the Continental Congress in Philadelphia, to “Remember the Ladies” in the new laws he expected Congress and the states to write, and he answered with a joke.

Remember too that the Revolution worked like a civil war within the colonies. Loyalists, Patriots, and the enslaved all made choices about whom to trust, and Black and Native Americans often sided with whoever seemed more likely to protect their freedom or land. As you read, ask whether each source shows a society being remade, a society holding on to its old order, or someone with no power trying to make the new nation keep its promises.

1776Case file 1 of 3
Who: Abigail Adams of Braintree, Massachusetts, and her husband John Adams, a delegate in Philadelphia
From: Two letters between husband and wife

A woman who runs a family farm writes to her husband as Congress moves toward independence, and he answers.

Abigail, March 31, 1776: . . . I desire you would Remember the Ladies, and be more generous and favourable to them than your ancestors. . . . If perticuliar care and attention is not paid to the Laidies we are determined to foment a Rebelion, and will not hold ourselves bound by any Laws in which we have no voice, or Representation. John, April 14, 1776: As to your extraordinary Code of Laws, I cannot but laugh. . . . Depend upon it, We know better than to repeal our Masculine systems.
Abigail Adams to John Adams, March 31, 1776, and John Adams to Abigail Adams, April 14, 1776, Adams Family Correspondence, vol. 1, Massachusetts Historical Society (Adams Papers Digital Edition). Spelling is original. Passages are joined by ellipses; the sentence beginning “If perticuliar care” follows the one before it in the letter.
Word help: Code of Laws = a new set of laws · generous and favourable = fair and kind · ancestors = earlier generations of men · foment = stir up · Rebelion = rebellion · Representation = having someone speak for you in government

1776–1778Case file 2 of 3
Who: The framers of the Pennsylvania (1776) and South Carolina (1778) state constitutions
From: Voting rules in two state constitutions

Two new states write rules for who may vote, and the answers are not the same.

Pennsylvania, 1776: Every freemen of the full age of twenty-one Years, . . . and paid public taxes during that time, shall enjoy the right of an elector: Provided always, that sons of freeholders of the age of twenty-one years shall be intitled to vote although they have not paid taxes. South Carolina, 1778: The qualification of electors shall be that every free white man, and no other person, who acknowledges the being of a God, . . . and hath a freehold at least of fifty acres of land, or a town lot, . . . shall be deemed a person qualified to vote
Constitution of Pennsylvania, September 28, 1776, Frame of Government, Sect. 6, and Constitution of South Carolina, March 19, 1778, Art. XIII, Avalon Project, Yale Law School. Spelling is original; ellipses mark cuts within the South Carolina article.
Word help: freemen = free men with full rights · elector = voter · freeholders = men who own land · intitled = entitled · freehold = owned land · qualified = allowed

1777Case file 3 of 3
Who: Prince Hall, a free Black man, and other enslaved Black men in Massachusetts
From: Petition to the state legislature, January 13, 1777

Black men held in bondage in a state that is fighting for liberty ask its lawmakers to end slavery.

That your Petitioners apprehend that they have, in common with all other Men, a natural and unalienable right to that freedom, which the great Parent of the Universe hath bestowed equally on all Mankind . . . & in defiance of all the tender feelings of humanity, brought hither to be sold like Beasts of Burthen, & like them condemned to slavery for Life . . . every principle from which America has acted in the course of her unhappy difficulties with Great-Britain, pleads stronger than a thousand arguments in favor of your Petitioners
Petition of Prince Hall and others to the Massachusetts General Court, January 13, 1777, Massachusetts Archives Collection, vol. 212, in Black Writers of the Founding Era (Library of America), via Library of America, Story of the Week. Spelling is original; the ellipses mark cuts.
Word help: Petitioners = people who formally ask the government for action · unalienable = cannot be taken away · Parent of the Universe = God · Beasts of Burthen = work animals · pleads = argues
