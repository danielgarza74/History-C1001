Part 1 · Background Readings

Debate 1 · Salem Witchcraft

Fear, Spite, and the Salem Trials

In the winter of 1692 several girls in Salem Village, Massachusetts, began to suffer violent fits, and a local doctor suggested that the cause might be supernatural. Under pressure, the girls named three women as witches: Tituba, an enslaved woman from Barbados, Sarah Osborne, and Sarah Good, a poor woman who begged for food and shelter. The magistrates John Hathorne and Jonathan Corwin questioned Good on March 1, 1692. Within a year more than 150 people had been arrested, and nineteen were hanged, including Good in July, before the governor closed the special court.

Belief in witchcraft was not strange in the seventeenth century. Puritan colonists believed that God and Satan were fighting a real war over New England, and ministers taught that the devil could work through human servants. Cotton Mather, a prominent Boston minister, defended the trials in The Wonders of the Invisible World, published in 1693. Court officials also accepted the girls’ claims that they saw the shapes of accused witches tormenting them, so an accusation could be hard to answer.

Historians have disagreed about what to make of this. Within a decade a critic named Robert Calef charged that the trials rested on fraud and that Mather’s book was propaganda. Many later scholars looked past the supernatural to social causes. Paul Boyer and Stephen Nissenbaum argued in 1974 that quarrels between Salem Village and wealthier Salem Town, and between neighbors, shaped who accused whom. Carol Karlsen showed that accused women were often older, outspoken, or in line to inherit property, so that the crisis was largely about women who stepped outside expected roles. Others, such as Chadwick Hansen in 1969, insisted that people truly believed in witchcraft, and some even practiced it, so that fear itself must be part of the story. Recent historians usually combine the explanations rather than choose one.

Sarah Good makes a good test case. She and her husband William had lost their property to the debts of her first husband, were homeless, and lived by renting rooms and asking neighbors for help. The couple stayed for a time with Samuel and Mary Abbey, who later turned them out. Good had a reputation for a sharp tongue, and she was hanged on July 19, 1692. As you read, ask whether each source shows people acting from sincere fear of witchcraft, from quarrels and prejudice, or from both at once.

Debate 2 · The Rise of Racial Slavery

Bacon’s Rebellion and the Rise of Racial Slavery

Virginia’s planters grew tobacco, a crop that demanded enormous amounts of land and labor. In the early decades most workers were English indentured servants, who agreed to labor for a set number of years in return for their passage. Africans had been present in the colony since 1619, but their legal status was not yet fixed. Black and white servants often worked side by side, sometimes ran away together, and were sometimes punished alike. By 1675 the English mainland colonies held only about 5,000 enslaved Africans. By the eve of the Revolution it had grown to roughly 500,000.

Historians have fought over how that change happened. In 1676 Nathaniel Bacon, a planter, led an armed rising against Governor William Berkeley. Bacon’s followers resented Berkeley’s Native American policy and the privileges of the governor’s circle, and they included poor farmers, indentured servants, and some enslaved Africans. The rebels burned Jamestown before Bacon died of illness and the revolt collapsed. Edmund Morgan and T. H. Breen argue that this multiracial uprising terrified the planter elite. Afterward, they say, leaders bought more enslaved Africans instead of servants and passed laws that separated Black and white people, giving poor whites rights and status so that they would side with planters, not with workers of African descent.

John Coombs disagrees. He argues that Virginia’s planters were already turning to slavery long before 1676, pulled by tobacco profits and by a growing Atlantic trade in people, and that neither the rebellion nor the later fall in the number of white servants had much to do with it. Kathleen Brown adds another lens: gender. When Virginia’s lawmakers decided in 1662 that a child’s status would follow the mother’s, they made enslaved women’s bodies the engine by which slavery reproduced itself. For her, the story of racial slavery cannot be told without women.

Several pieces of evidence matter here. In 1640 a Virginia court punished three runaway servants, two white and one Black. In 1676 the colony’s lawmakers, the House of Burgesses, wrote to the king to explain the rebellion. And across the century Virginia lawmakers passed a growing body of laws about enslaved people, including a harsh law in 1680 after the rebellion. Ask whether each source suggests that the rebellion was a turning point, that the turn had already happened, or that something else was at work.

Debate 3 · Origins of the Revolution

Ideas or Hardship? Why the Colonists Rebelled

After the French and Indian War ended in 1763, Britain was deeply in debt and decided that the colonies should help pay for the empire. Parliament passed the Sugar Act in 1764 and, in 1765, the Stamp Act, the first direct tax it had ever laid on the colonies. Colonial assemblies and the Stamp Act Congress answered that only their own legislatures could tax them. Crowds took to the streets as well. In Boston in August 1765 a mob destroyed the house of Andrew Oliver, the man appointed to collect the stamp tax, and later ransacked the home of Lieutenant Governor Thomas Hutchinson. Francis Bernard, the royal governor of Massachusetts, reported the riots to the Lords of Trade in London, the board that advised the Crown on colonial affairs.

Parliament repealed the Stamp Act in 1766 but kept looking for revenue. In 1767 the Townshend Acts taxed imports such as glass, paper, and tea. That same year Parliament suspended the New York Assembly for refusing to supply British troops as the Quartering Act required, and a Pennsylvania lawyer and landowner named John Dickinson answered with Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania, essays that were reprinted across the colonies.

Historians have long asked why so many colonists risked war. In 1967 Bernard Bailyn argued in The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution that the answer lies in ideas. After reading hundreds of pamphlets, he concluded that words like “slavery,” “corruption,” and “conspiracy” were not just propaganda. Colonists drew on British opposition thinkers and truly feared a plot by distant officials to destroy their liberty, and that fear lay at the heart of the Revolution. Gary Nash, writing in 1976, pushed back. He argued that studying only the pamphlets of merchants and lawyers overlooks the lives of ordinary city dwellers. In Boston, New York, and Philadelphia, rising poverty and growing gaps between rich and poor made artisans and laborers angry, and a popular ideology about wealth and power joined the elite arguments about constitutional rights.

Both historians agree that colonists were angry and that ideas mattered. They differ over where to look and over who was driving events. As you read the two letters, notice who is speaking, who the speaker is afraid of, and whether the writer is worried about the loss of liberty, about the crowd, or about both.

A shunned, homeless woman is questioned in Salem Village after children claim she is tormenting them.

(H) desired the children all of them to look upon her, and see, if this were the person that had hurt them and so they all did looke upon her and said this was one of the persons that did torment them presently they were all tormented. (H) Sarah good doe you not see now what you have done why doe you not tell us the truth, why doe you thus torment these poor children . . . he answered no not in this nature but it was her bad carriage to him and indeed said he I may say with tears that shee is an enimy to all good.

A royal governor reports to London after crowds in Boston attack the homes of Stamp Act officials.
